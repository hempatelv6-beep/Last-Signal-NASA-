## Part 2: Equipment profiles

### 🌕 THE MOON

#### Surveyor 3 (robotic lander)
1. **What:** a three-legged robot lander with a TV camera and a scoop. It tested whether the Moon's soil could hold up a spacecraft.
2. **Mission:** Surveyor program (NASA/JPL), 1966–68.
3. **Why sent:** before Apollo, nobody knew whether a lander would sink into the dust. Surveyor answered that.
4. **Arrived:** launched 17 April 1967; landed 20 April 1967 in Oceanus Procellarum (the Ocean of Storms). **[V]**
5. **Accomplished:** its engines didn't cut off, so it **bounced twice** before settling **[V]**. It dug trenches with its scoop and sent back thousands of TV images **[K]**.
6. **Operational?** No. It stopped operating on 4 May 1967 **[V: key-dates list]**.
7. **Why it stopped:** the lunar night (about 14 Earth days of extreme cold) **[K]**.
8. **Where now:** still there. **Apollo 12 astronauts walked to it in November 1969**, cut off its camera and other parts, and brought them home so engineers could study 2.5 years of space exposure **[V: NASA mission page photo caption; K: details]**.
9. **Why the story is great:** it's the **only robot that humans later visited on another world**. A bacteria found in the returned camera was famously reported as "surviving on the Moon"; later analysis suggests it was probably contamination on Earth **[K]**. That makes a great "how science corrects itself" lesson.
- Source: [NASA Science: Surveyor 3](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/surveyor-3/) · [NASA: Apollo 12](https://www.nasa.gov/mission/apollo-12/)

#### Apollo Lunar Module descent stages (6 of them)
1. **What:** the bottom half of each Apollo lander, with its legs, engine and fuel tanks.
2. **Mission:** Apollo 11, 12, 14, 15, 16, 17 (1969–1972).
3. **Why sent:** to land two astronauts softly.
4. **Arrived:** Apollo 11 "Eagle" on 20 July 1969 at Tranquility Base **[K]**.
5. **Accomplished:** landed 12 people on the Moon, then **served as the launch pad** for the top half (ascent stage) to blast off **[K]**.
6. **Operational?** No, and it was never designed to work after launch.
7. **Why left:** every kilogram lifted off the Moon costs fuel. Leaving the heavy bottom half behind was the plan from the start **[K]**.
8. **Where now:** all six sites; NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter (LRO) has photographed them from orbit, including astronaut footpaths **[K]**.
9. **Why interesting:** "abandoned on purpose" is a different kind of story from "it broke".
- Source: [NASA: Apollo 11](https://www.nasa.gov/mission/apollo-11/) · [LROC Apollo site images](https://www.lroc.asu.edu/)

#### Lunar Roving Vehicles (LRV-1, -2, -3)
1. **What:** battery-powered, folding "Moon buggies" that carried two astronauts.
2. **Mission:** Apollo 15, 16, 17 **[V]**.
3. **Why sent:** to let astronauts travel much farther from the lander and collect more varied rocks **[V]**.
4. **Arrived:** 1971 (Apollo 15) to 1972 (Apollo 17).
5. **Accomplished:** each was driven on three traverses, one per day **[V]**. Apollo 17's rover covered roughly 36 km in total **[K]**.
6. **Operational?** No.
7. **Why left:** too heavy to bring home; the Lunar Module had no room for it.
8. **Where now:** parked at Hadley–Apennine, Descartes and Taurus–Littrow. They were often parked where a TV camera could film the liftoff **[K]**.
9. **Why interesting:** three "cars parked on the Moon"; a relatable story for kids.
- Source: [NSSDCA: Apollo LRV](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/lunar/apollo_lrv.html)

#### Laser Ranging Retroreflectors (Apollo 11, 14, 15)
1. **What:** panels of special "corner-cube" mirrors that bounce light straight back to where it came from.
2. **Mission:** placed by Apollo 11 (1969), 14 and 15 (1971) **[K]**.
3. **Why sent:** observatories fire lasers at them and time the echo, which measures the Earth–Moon distance very precisely.
4. **Arrived:** 21 July 1969 for Apollo 11.
5. **Accomplished:** showed the **Moon is moving away from Earth by about 3.8 cm per year**, and tests Einstein's theory of gravity **[K]**.
6. **Operational?** ✅ **Yes, still used, 57 years later.** It's passive: no power needed.
7. **Why still working:** it has no electronics to fail.
8. **Where now:** Tranquility Base, Fra Mauro and Hadley Rille.
9. **Why interesting:** **"abandoned" but still doing science.** It's the perfect counter-example to "left behind = useless".
- Source: [NASA Science: Lunar Laser Ranging](https://science.nasa.gov/moon/lunar-laser-ranging/)

#### ALSEP: Apollo Lunar Surface Experiments Packages (Apollo 12, 14, 15, 16, 17)
1. **What:** nuclear-powered science stations with seismometers, heat-flow probes, magnetometers and more.
2. **Why sent:** to "listen" to the Moon's interior and measure its environment for years after the astronauts left.
3. **Accomplished:** recorded thousands of moonquakes and meteorite impacts **[K]**.
4. **Operational?** No. **NASA switched them off on 30 September 1977, largely for budget reasons** **[K]**.
5. **Why interesting:** they didn't break; **people decided to stop listening**. That's a thought-provoking story. Most competitors skip it.
- Source: [NASA: Apollo Lunar Surface Journal](https://www.nasa.gov/history/alsj/)

#### Personal and "human" items
Alan Shepard's golf balls (Apollo 14), the "Fallen Astronaut" memorial figure (Apollo 15), Charlie Duke's family photo (Apollo 16), and bags of waste left to save weight **[K]**. These are good for curiosity hooks, but say "about 96 bags" only if you can cite a source.

#### Crashed on purpose: impact missions
Ranger 7 (1964, first close-up Moon photos before impact), **LCROSS (2009, slammed a rocket stage into a polar crater and found water ice)**, GRAIL "Ebb and Flow" (2012) and LADEE (2014) **[K]**. These are "abandoned" as craters, not as machines, which is a fresh angle almost no one uses.

### 🔴 MARS

#### Viking 1 & Viking 2 landers
1. **What:** the first successful US Mars landers, each paired with an orbiter.
2. **Why sent:** to photograph the surface, study weather, and run the **first experiments to look for life**.
3. **Arrived:** Viking 1 launched 20 Aug 1975 and landed 20 July 1976 **[V]**. Viking 2 landed 3 Sept 1976 **[V]**.
4. **Accomplished:** first pictures from the surface of Mars, years of weather data, and biology experiments whose results are still debated **[K]**.
5. **Operational?** No. Viking 2 ended in April 1980 (batteries); Viking 1 in November 1982, after a faulty command cut off contact **[K]**.
6. **Where now:** Chryse Planitia (Viking 1) and Utopia Planitia (Viking 2).
7. **Why interesting:** Viking 1 was lost because of **a human software mistake**, not wear. 2026 is also Viking's **50th anniversary**: NASA has a "Viking: 50 Years on Mars" page **[V]**, so this is a timely hook!
- Source: [NASA: Viking 1](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/viking-1/) · [NASA: Viking 2](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/viking-2/)

#### Mars Pathfinder lander & Sojourner rover
- Launched 4 Dec 1996; landed **4 July 1997** in Ares Vallis, using airbags; mission ended 27 Sept 1997 **[V]**.
- Sojourner was the **first wheeled rover on another planet**, about the size of a microwave oven **[V]: "first-ever robotic rover"; [K]: size**.
- It stopped because the lander, which relayed Sojourner's signals, lost contact (most likely its battery) **[K]**.
- **Why interesting:** a tiny "test" rover that started the whole Mars rover era.
- Source: [NASA: Mars Pathfinder](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/mars-pathfinder/)

#### Spirit rover (MER-A)
- Landed January 2004 in Gusev Crater **[K]**. It was planned for 90 sols, like Opportunity.
- **Got stuck in soft soil on 1 May 2009** **[V]**. A broken front wheel had been dragging for years, and that dragging accidentally **uncovered bright silica**, evidence of ancient hot springs or steam vents **[V]**.
- Silent since March 2010; NASA ended contact attempts in May 2011 **[V]**. It couldn't tilt its solar panels toward the Sun for winter.
- **Why interesting:** its **broken wheel made a discovery**. A great "failure leads to science" story.
- Source: [NASA: Spirit](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/mer-spirit/)

#### Opportunity rover (MER-B)
- Landed January 2004 in Meridiani Planum **[K]**. **Planned for 90 sols, about three Earth months** **[V]**.
- Drove **more than 28 miles (45 km)**, setting the off-world driving record, and ended in Perseverance Valley **[V]**.
- Found hematite "blueberries" and other evidence of **past liquid water** **[K]**.
- **Stopped communicating after a planet-wide dust storm in June 2018** **[V]**. NASA declared the mission over in February 2019 **[K]**.
- **Why interesting:** the most famous story, and that's exactly the problem. Every team uses it. If you include it, find a less obvious angle.
- Source: [NASA: Opportunity](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/mer-opportunity/)

#### Phoenix lander
- Landed May 2008 in the Martian **arctic** **[K]**. It dug into the ground and **confirmed water ice** just below the surface **[V]**.
- Its last signal was in November 2008, as polar winter came. It wasn't designed to survive the winter, and orbiter images later showed its solar panels had broken under ice **[K]**.
- **Why interesting:** it was **built knowing it would freeze**. A "mission with an expiry date".
- Source: [NASA: Phoenix](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/phoenix/)

#### InSight lander
- Landed November 2018 in Elysium Planitia **[K]**. It put a seismometer on Mars and **detected about 1,319 marsquakes** **[K]**.
- Its "mole" heat probe could not dig down as planned, because the soil didn't give it enough grip **[K]**.
- **Dust slowly covered its solar panels.** Last contact 15 Dec 2022; NASA ended the mission in December 2022 **[K]**. NASA has since used orbiter images of the retired lander to study dust movement **[V: headline on NASA page]**.
- **Why interesting:** it gave us the **first map of the inside of another planet** (crust, mantle, core).
- Source: [NASA: InSight](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/insight/)

#### Ingenuity Mars Helicopter
- Rode along with Perseverance. **First powered, controlled flight on another planet: 19 April 2021** **[V]**.
- **Designed for at most 5 flights in 30 days; completed 72** **[V]**.
- **Last flight on 18 January 2024** **[V]**. A rotor blade was damaged, so it can no longer fly **[V: page mentions rotor damage; K: details]**.
- **Still there**, in Neretva Vallis inside Jezero Crater **[K]**.
- **Why interesting:** a "tech demo" that turned into a scout, and a helicopter flying in air about 1% as thick as Earth's **[K]**.
- Source: [NASA: Ingenuity](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/mars-2020-perseverance/ingenuity-mars-helicopter/)

#### Entry, Descent & Landing (EDL) leftovers: heat shields, parachutes, backshells, sky cranes
- Every Mars landing scatters hardware: the **heat shield** (which protects during entry), the **parachute and backshell**, and, for Curiosity and Perseverance, the **sky crane** that lowered the rover, then flew away and crashed on purpose **[K]**.
- Ingenuity and orbiters have **photographed this debris** **[K]**.
- **Why interesting:** "trash" that tells you **how you land on Mars**. **Almost no competitor uses this.**
- Source: [NASA Mars 2020 mission](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/mars-2020-perseverance/) · [HiRISE](https://www.uahirise.org/)

#### Perseverance's sample tubes (a "left behind on purpose" depot)
- Perseverance has **deliberately set down sealed rock-sample tubes on the surface** as a backup depot, intended for a future return mission **[K]**.
- **Why interesting:** hardware left behind **for the future**, not the past. The return mission's status has been uncertain, so check the latest NASA news before you write about it.

#### Mars Polar Lander (lost in 1999)
- Contact was lost during landing in December 1999. The most likely cause is that its engines shut off too early, after a landing-leg sensor gave a false "touchdown" signal **[K]**. It was never found for certain.
- **Why interesting:** a **"missing" machine**. Not every story ends with a known resting place.

### Still working (not abandoned)
**Curiosity** (launched 26 Nov 2011, landed 6 Aug 2012, Gale Crater **[V]**) and **Perseverance** are still active. **Voyager 1 & 2** are still sending data from interstellar space **[K]**. Label these clearly as active. One competitor wrongly listed Curiosity and Perseverance as "permanent hardware".
